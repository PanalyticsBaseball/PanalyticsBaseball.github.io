## Executive Summary

Bubba Chandler made a substantial in-season mechanical adjustment. His average arm angle declined from 46.8 degrees during the high-slot phase to 38.7 degrees during the sustained low-slot phase. The lower slot improved strike throwing: BB% declined from 13.3% to 9.8%, Zone% increased from 46.9% to 50.6%, and CSW% increased from 25.2% to 26.5%. That CSW gain came from called strikes rather than additional bat-missing ability.

The adjustment did not produce more deception. Whiff% declined from 22.3% to 17.4%, Chase% fell from 30.5% to 25.7%, and Hard-Hit% increased from 36.0% to 41.7%. Chandler's slider became 3.2 mph harder and gained vertical movement, but its Whiff% declined from 35.4% to 12.5%. The pitch became more useful for acquiring strikes and less effective for ending plate appearances.

Chandler's raw four-seam traits remained stable within starts, but his results deteriorated as hitters saw him again. xwOBA increased from .289 in a hitter's First PA to .329 in the Second PA and .356 in the Third+ PA. This points more strongly toward pitch recognition, location, and sequencing than simple velocity fatigue. His current profile supports a starter development path, but the third-time-through plan and breaking-ball identity remain unresolved.

## 1. When Did the Arm Slot Change?

The change was gradual rather than a single-game event. Chandler operated primarily around 45–51 degrees through mid-June, transitioned toward 40 degrees between June 19 and July 22, and stabilized near 38–39 degrees from July 28 through September 13. His final two starts moved back toward 42 degrees, creating a small late-rebound sample worth monitoring.

The adjustment appeared across the entire arsenal. From the high-slot period to the low-slot period, four-seam arm angle declined by approximately seven degrees, slider arm angle by six degrees, and changeup arm angle by seven degrees. Extension remained close to 6.6 feet, indicating that the change came primarily from release geometry rather than a shorter stride.

## 2. Did the Lower Slot Improve Dominance?

The lower slot improved control but did not make Chandler more difficult to hit. The high-slot system generated the strongest combination of K%, Whiff%, and Chase%. The low-slot system produced the best BB%, Zone%, and CSW%, but its called-strike rate increased while Whiff% declined.

### Four-system conclusions

- **High slot produced the most swing-and-miss pressure.** It led the four systems in K% and Chase% and led the three meaningful samples in Whiff%. Its limitation was inefficient strike throwing, reflected in the highest BB% and lowest Zone%.
- **Transition produced the best outcomes without the best underlying dominance.** Its .286 xwOBA and 86.7 mph average exit velocity were the best marks, but its 15.6% K% and 23.4% CSW% suggest that some of the run prevention came from favorable contact outcomes rather than a fully optimized arsenal.
- **Low slot produced the clearest command improvement.** It delivered the lowest BB%, highest Zone%, and highest CSW%. However, the CSW profile shifted toward called strikes: Called Strike% reached 18.8%, while Whiff% fell to 17.4% and Chase% to 25.7%.
- **The low slot did not suppress damaging contact.** Soft contact increased to 29.5%, but Hard-Hit% also rose to 41.7% and xwOBA deteriorated to .333. The system created more extremes rather than uniformly weaker contact.
- **Late rebound is an experiment, not a verdict.** Whiff% recovered to 23.6% and Zone% remained near 49%, but the phase includes only two games and 38 PA. Its 50.0% Hard-Hit% and .347 xwOBA prevent a positive conclusion from that sample.

The four-system comparison identifies a development target rather than a clear winner: preserve the low-slot system's strike efficiency while recovering the high-slot system's chase and swing-and-miss qualities. The transition range near 41–44 degrees remains the most logical testing window, but its favorable xwOBA should not be treated as proof of superior dominance.

Contact quality was also mixed. Soft contact increased, but Hard-Hit% moved in the same direction. The transition phase produced the best xwOBA at .286, while the sustained low-slot phase produced .333 xwOBA.

The data do not identify the lowest slot as the optimal solution. The more promising target may be the 41–44 degree range used during the transition and late-rebound periods, but the two-start rebound sample is not sufficient for a firm conclusion.

## 3. How Did the Breaking-Ball System Change?

The slider changed identity as the slot dropped. It became harder, gained induced vertical break, lost horizontal movement, and moved into the strike zone more frequently. Its profile moved closer to a cutter or hard slider.

<figure class="pb-report-figure">
  <img class="pb-report-chart" src="{{ '/assets/img/player-analysis/bubba-chandler/04-breaking-ball-shape.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Bubba Chandler breaking-ball movement and usage by arm-slot phase">
  <figcaption>The sweeper largely disappeared, the slider became harder and more vertical, and the curveball gained a larger role.</figcaption>
</figure>

The change created a tradeoff:

- Slider velocity increased from 89.5 to 92.7 mph.
- Slider IVB increased from 2.0 to 6.1 inches.
- Slider Whiff% declined from 35.4% to 12.5%.
- Slider xwOBA increased from .298 to .367.
- Sweeper usage declined from 7.8% to 0.2%.
- Curveball usage increased from 2.7% to 7.7%.

The sweeper was not simply relabeled as a slider. Its horizontal movement remained clearly distinct. Chandler replaced a lateral bat-missing pitch with a harder, more strike-oriented breaking ball.

## 4. How Did Slider Location Change?

The high-slot slider was concentrated below the zone and near the lower glove-side edge. The low-slot version moved upward and into the strike zone. Zone% increased, but the pitch became easier to contact.

<figure class="pb-report-figure">

</figure>

The development question is not whether Chandler should abandon the hard slider. It is whether the organization can separate its roles: use the hard slider for strikes and preserve a second breaking ball with enough depth or sweep to finish hitters.

## 5. Is the Decline Caused by Fatigue?

Four-seam velocity and induced vertical break remained stable across pitch-count buckets. Velocity averaged between 98.1 and 98.4 mph, and IVB remained between 17.2 and 17.4 inches. Arm angle declined modestly, but the raw fastball did not lose meaningful velocity or vertical movement.

The largest performance deterioration occurred between pitches 51 and 75, when BB% reached 15.2% and xwOBA increased to .354. Because four-seam velocity and shape remained intact, location and hitter familiarity deserve more attention than raw fatigue.

## 6. What Happens When Hitters See Him Again?

Chandler was substantially better in a hitter's First PA. His K% declined in the Second PA, while walks and expected damage increased through the Third+ PA.

<figure class="pb-report-figure">
  <img class="pb-report-chart" src="{{ '/assets/img/player-analysis/bubba-chandler/05-results-by-hitter-exposure.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Bubba Chandler strikeout walk and xwOBA results by hitter plate appearance exposure">

This is not proof that Chandler cannot remain a starter. It indicates that his current arsenal presentation does not sustain its advantage. The organization should test whether a more differentiated third-look plan can reduce the gap before changing his role.

## 7. Which Sequences Work Best?

The most effective frequent sequence was four-seam followed by slider. The second pitch in that sequence generated 27.9% Whiff, 41.9% Chase, and .226 xwOBA. Reversing the pair was much less effective: slider followed by four-seam generated 16.1% Whiff and .363 xwOBA.

<figure class="pb-report-figure">
  <img class="pb-report-chart" src="{{ '/assets/img/player-analysis/bubba-chandler/07-sequence-effectiveness.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Bubba Chandler pitch-pair effectiveness by whiff rate xwOBA and frequency">
  <figcaption>The direction of the sequence matters. The slider plays better after the four-seam than the four-seam plays after the slider.</figcaption>
</figure>

Repeated changeups also produced a useful profile: 25.6% Whiff and .257 xwOBA. This gives Chandler another way to avoid a predictable breaking-ball-to-fastball pattern.

Sequence results are descriptive rather than causal. Count, location, batter side, and selection effects influence each pair. They nevertheless identify combinations worth testing more deliberately.

## 8. Does He Have a Two-Strike Finisher?

Chandler has several usable two-strike pitches but no single dominant option. The four-seam and changeup produced strong expected results, while the slider generated the most balanced combination of usage and Whiff%. The sweeper's 30.0% Whiff and .182 xwOBA are encouraging but come from only 39 pitches.

<figure class="pb-report-figure">
  <img class="pb-report-chart" src="{{ '/assets/img/player-analysis/bubba-chandler/08-two-strike-pitch-options.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Bubba Chandler two-strike pitch usage whiff rate and xwOBA">
  <figcaption>The two-strike plan should use multiple shapes, but it needs a breaking ball that can consistently produce misses below the zone.</figcaption>
</figure>

The hard slider's two-strike value declined after the arm-slot change. Restoring depth to one breaking ball is a higher priority than simply increasing breaking-ball usage.

## 9. How Should the Pitch Mix Change by Batter Side?

Chandler allowed .296 xwOBA against right-handed hitters and .335 against left-handed hitters. Right-handed hitters also chased more frequently, 30.5% compared with 26.2% for left-handed hitters.

<figure class="pb-report-figure">
  <img class="pb-report-chart" src="{{ '/assets/img/player-analysis/bubba-chandler/10-pitch-plan-by-batter-side.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Bubba Chandler pitch usage whiff rate and xwOBA by batter side">
  <figcaption>The current pitch mix does not fully reflect which pitches performed best against each batter side.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two opportunities stand out:

- Against right-handed hitters, the changeup produced 35.1% Whiff and .192 xwOBA despite only 6.9% usage. The sweeper produced 31.3% Whiff and .216 xwOBA in a smaller sample.
- Against left-handed hitters, the four-seam produced .353 xwOBA and the sinker .403. The changeup generated a more competitive .306 xwOBA, but its location and shape still need refinement.

The organization should test more changeups against right-handed hitters and reconsider whether completely removing the sweeper gave away a useful platoon weapon.

## Development and Usage Recommendations

### 1. Test a controlled 41–44 degree arm-slot target

The lowest slot improved control but reduced deception. The transition range produced the best expected results, and the late rebound recovered Whiff% in a very small sample. A controlled trial should compare pitch shape, command, and health feedback rather than treating the lowest repeatable slot as automatically optimal.

### 2. Separate strike-getting and finishing breaking balls

Retain the hard slider as an in-zone strike pitch. Develop the curveball or a reintroduced sweeper as the chase and finishing shape. The objective is functional separation, not adding another label to the arsenal.

### 3. Build the plan around effective directionality

Prioritize four-seam → slider and selected changeup repetitions. Reduce predictable slider → four-seam transitions unless location or count creates a specific advantage.

### 4. Create different plans for right- and left-handed hitters

Expand changeup trials against right-handed hitters. Against left-handed hitters, review elevated four-seam command, reduce noncompetitive sinker usage, and identify whether the curve can provide a second below-zone weapon.

### 5. Manage the third look while development continues

Chandler's present profile is strongest during the first trip through the lineup. Until the third-look plan improves, earlier matchup-based removal may protect results without abandoning starter development.

## Bottom Line

Chandler's lower arm slot made his delivery more repeatable and improved strike throwing, but it did not increase dominance. The adjustment changed the slider from a bat-missing pitch into a harder strike pitch and reduced overall chase and whiff. His four-seam stuff remains strong deep into starts, so the most actionable path is to improve breaking-ball separation, sequence direction, and the plan for repeated hitter exposure.

The starter foundation remains: premium fastball velocity, multiple secondary shapes, and evidence that control can improve. The next developmental step is not more raw stuff. It is organizing that stuff into a plan that remains unpredictable beyond the First PA.

## Methodology and Limitations

The study uses Baseball Savant pitch-by-pitch data from March 31 through September 25, 2026. Whiff% is misses divided by swings. Chase% is swings at pitches outside the Statcast zone. Soft contact is defined as batted balls below 80 mph, and Hard-Hit% as batted balls at 95 mph or harder. Mechanical phases follow sustained game-level arm-angle changes.

Pitch-level expected results, sequence splits, and late-season phases include different sample sizes. Sequence results are influenced by count, location, batter quality, and selection. The analysis identifies development hypotheses and testing priorities; it does not establish that the arm-slot change caused every downstream result.
